# From zero to the partial derivatives and JVP used by MeanFlow

This page covers only the calculus MeanFlow actually needs, without turning into a full multivariable-calculus course.

<CalculusJVPPrimer lang="en" />

## Geometric intuition: a direction is not an abstract symbol

<DistributionFlowLab lang="en" />

The Conditional → Marginal tab shows multiple sample-specific directions, while the Average velocity tab separates the instantaneous tangent from the finite-interval average. A JVP asks how a network output changes when its input moves along such a direction.

## 1. Why partial derivatives appear

MeanFlow models

$$
u=u(z_t,r,t).
$$

When asking how $u$ changes with $t$, both $t$ and the state $z_t$ move, giving

$$
\frac{du}{dt}=\partial_z u\,\frac{dz_t}{dt}+\partial_tu.
$$

Since $dz_t/dt=v$,

$$
\frac{du}{dt}=\partial_z u\,v+\partial_tu.
$$

## 2. Jacobian and JVP

When $z$ and $u$ are high-dimensional, $\partial_z u$ is a Jacobian matrix. MeanFlow does not need the whole matrix, only its product with the flow direction. That is the Jacobian-vector product.

## 3. Why the tangent is $[v,0,1]$

For input $(z,r,t)$, state moves at velocity $v$, $r$ is held fixed, and $t$ advances at unit speed:

$$
\operatorname{JVP}(u;v)=J_{u(z,r,t)}[v,0,1]=\partial_z u\,v+\partial_tu.
$$

## 4. iMF notation reminder

$$
\operatorname{JVP}(u_\theta;v_\theta)
$$

does **not** mean differentiating $v_\theta$. The differentiated function is $u_\theta$; $v_\theta$ supplies the state-space tangent direction. The JVP output is then assembled into capital

$$
V_\theta=u_\theta+(t-r)\operatorname{JVP}_{sg}(u_\theta;v_\theta).
$$
